01The whole idea in five rules

A hub is a creative space owned by its members, who pay modest monthly dues and share in what it earns. Five rules govern everything, and none of them needs software to understand:

  1. 1Work is credited by peers, not bosses. Every contribution, from directing and editing to sound and building shelves, is logged with its hours. At least three independent reviewers score its quality (never yourself, never your own project's collaborators), and the median counts. Your merit is hours × that median. Nothing about who you are enters the math.
  2. 2Bills come first. Always. Nothing is profit until every expense of the month is paid, and any debts from past months are settled next. A hub that lost money pays out nothing, to anyone. That single rule is why this co-op can't die the way co-ops usually die: by distributing money it needed for rent.
  3. 3Payouts depend on how safe the hub is. The hub's health is its runway, the number of months it could survive on reserves. A struggling hub automatically saves; a safe hub automatically shares. Nobody votes on it, so nobody can be pressured over it.
  4. 4When healthy, profit splits four ways. Your share of the members' half is your merit divided by everyone's merit. If a project earns money years later, the same ledger recomputes the same shares: residuals forever, no negotiating, no lost paperwork.
  5. 5Growth is earned, never gambled. A hub may seed a new hub in another city only with six-plus months of runway and three consecutive profitable, healthy months, and only from its expansion fund, never from reserves. One bad month resets the clock. Every hub's finances are firewalled, and every hub records who seeded it, forever.

02A month in the life of a hub

  1. Money comes in: dues, stage rentals, project services. Money goes out: rent, gear, wages. Every entry is recorded as it happens.
  2. Members log their work; peers review it through the month.
  3. On closing day the books settle in strict order: expenses, then old debts, then the 1% fee on what's left, then the health-gated split.
  4. The month's report (revenue, costs, health, runway, every payout) goes to all members with a conservation audit: a check that every unit of money in equals every unit out or held. It's the meeting agenda, and there's nothing to argue about because anyone can recompute it.
  5. Repeat. When runway crosses six months and holds, full payouts begin. When the expansion fund can cover it, the next city gets its studio.

04Why you can trust it

  • Everything is deterministic. Same ledger, same results, byte for byte: scores, payouts, sponsor rankings, audits. Any member can recompute any decision.
  • It's adversarially tested. 91 tests, including a dedicated attack suite: shill reviews, review rings, double votes, hour-cap probing, rounding attacks at extreme values, ledger tampering, bleed-the-hub-dry sequences, and simulated decades of boom and bust checked for exact conservation after every period. Some attacks found real flaws during development; the attacks and the fixes are both in the repository.
  • All money is exact integer arithmetic. No floating-point drift; rounding remainders go to reserves by design.
  • It can't be taken private. AGPL-3.0: every deployment, including hosted ones, must publish its source.

05And the honest limits

A system that claims to fix trust owes you these up front:

  • Code can't see your bank account. The audit proves the books are internally exact, not that entries match reality. The founding guide mandates monthly bank reconciliation by a rotating member, never the bookkeeper.
  • A majority of colluding reviewers beats the median. One shill can't move your score; an organized majority can. Small hubs should twin review pools with another hub. Capture is made costly and detectable, not impossible.
  • Hours are attested, not surveilled. Caps and peer review bound the damage, but a community that tolerates inflated hours has a culture problem no ledger fixes.
  • The ledger hash isn't cryptographic yet. Today it catches accidental corruption and casual tampering. SHA-256 plus external anchoring is the designed upgrade; until then it's a smoke detector, not a vault.
  • Code isn't law until your bylaws say so. Phase 2 of the founding guide exists to close exactly this gap.

06The platform fee, disclosed in full

The reference deployment sends 1% of net profit (never revenue; loss months pay nothing) to the project's creator to fund development. It's visible in source, computed by the same exact math as every other flow, and zero unless your hub actually profits. It isn't technically immutable: this is AGPL code and a fork can strip it in one line. Keep it and you fund the commons this came from; fork it out and the licence permits that. 07What's real and what's planned

  • Working and tested now: the entire economic rule system, an 11-crate Rust workspace with the adversarial test suite.
  • Simulated, pending integration: XRPL transaction submission (amounts computed and logged, not yet sent), the DeFi treasury model, compliance adapters.
  • Designed, not started: the member dashboard, Substrate governance anchoring, IPFS storage.

The economic core came first deliberately: it's the part the industry gets wrong on purpose, and it had to be provably right before anything touches real money.
